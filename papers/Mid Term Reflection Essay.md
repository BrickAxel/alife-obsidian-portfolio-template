Artificial life is a vast field covering many topics. This makes sense when trying to define the realm of life as it could be. The most important part of any artificial life system is emergence.  In broad terms, emergence is a complex system that arises from simple systems that could not exist in any individual simple system. To be more specific, local interactions result in larger global behaviors. From what I have seen in this class, any system that could theoretically have complex enough behavior to raise questions about soft vs hard Alife must have emergent behavior. By that I mean, in order for a system to be sufficiently complex to create something that will have enough lifelike characteristics to potentially be considered alive, a series of smaller, simpler systems will be needed to create the complex system because a system that tries to start off complex will create a search space large enough to be near impossible to search and will be prone to having edge cases that cause systems to collapse.  

A lot of time can be spent discussing the best medium for trying to create an Alife system. While this will vary depending on the desired outcome, I am partial to cellular automata. It's easy to implement, has many ways of adding additional forms of complexity that can lead to emergent structures, and it creates visuals that are easy to understand in lower dimensions. Ease of implementation is important not just because of the time it takes but also in automating the search. The additional ways of adding complexity are important for creating larger search spaces. Visuals are important for allowing additional understanding and analysis. That being said, it does run headfirst into the curse of dimensionality.  This can be avoided in low density systems, systems with limited size, or an overwhelming amount of processing power (or a lot of patience). Even in a 1d space cellular automata can have interesting transient states and cycles given a moderate lambda and a few states per cell [[1D Cellular Automata Jupiter notebook]]. This brings up an interesting line what can be considered alive. In theory, with a large enough number of states, a 2d cellular automata could be compressed into a 1d cellular automata. The easiest way would just be a near 1 to 1 conversion of old dimensions to new states. When this is done, if there was some semblance of Alife in 2d it may not be visible in 1d. Not because it is impossible to detect in theory, rather, the challenge lies in the ability to recognize a 1d life form in comparison to background noise. It seems like this could be possible in other mediums or even vice versa. Our ability to detect Alife is limited to our ability to see it in mediums that make sense to us. Even when the search is automated, the automation searching the open-ended spaces can only look for things it was trained to look for. The search for life as it could be is limited to life as we can perceive and interpret it.  

That's not to say we shouldn't search at all or that we should never tinker with Alife. Just an acknowledgement that we are searching without truly knowing what we are looking for. Life is a very broad term, and I fear we, as people, are unable to adequately articulate what makes something alive. This will inherently cause issues when trying to search for Alife. If we were to take some of the basics, say self-replication and mutation, as the minimum needed for a system to be alive, then the classic fork bomb `:(){ :|:& };:` on a computer sitting in the sun counts (assuming the light could potentially flip a bit) and so is the 'bacteria' covered bacterial GA [[Week 4 Reflections]].  I'm not going to say this is or is not alive in some capacity. If this is alive, the definition survives, if it's not, a new one is needed. In this case the definition is weak but self-replication and mutation tend to be a small part of most definitions of life. Most definitions involving replication will inherently avoid non-replicating but immortal cells. While a cell spontaneously coming into existence with these properties is unlikely, one could theoretically be designed. Replication and mutation don't make something more or less alive; they just allow for evolution. A human without any DNA would still be alive, even if only for a brief time. While it would not be a long time, it would be approximately equivalent to extreme radiation poisoning, without additional cellular damage but missing some additional cellular regulators. Realistically this would cause death in hours or even minutes but initially wouldn’t be noticed. To the best of my knowledge, a person would still be fully conscious. While I don’t know of any specific rules that state something has to be alive to be conscious, I feel it is generally implied. Broadly I would define life as creating order from chaos. Starting from in a brief moment of spontaneous order, that allows more order to develop. The order it creates doesn't necessarily have to look like it. In this sense a glider gun could be considered alive, pushing an infinite number of stable states out for as long as it remains intact, while balancing on the edge of chaos, though it may be more realistic to view it as a catalyst. Yet even with this definition I feel unsatisfied.  

I still feel a bias pulling me towards reality. I think part of me wants to call the distinction between Life and not life arbitrary. A series of definitions made by humans to try and categorize an uncategorizable world. Perhaps that's why I feel a pull towards AChems close to chem and wet Alife. I still feel like moving between levels of abstraction could be very useful for chemistry simulations [[3-31-2025 worklog-notes]]. However, even with the best processors I have access to, if the goal is purely to create life, I think wet AChem will run faster because there is no run time, just experiment length.  Soft AChem does have it's advantages though, software can store more prior states and should theoretically be fully repeatable.
